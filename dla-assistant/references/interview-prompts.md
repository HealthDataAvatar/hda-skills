# DLA functional screening and interview prompts

Use these as **memory aids**. Never ask every question automatically. Never infer a need from a diagnosis.

Run a breadth-first screen first; deep-dive only into flagged areas.

Every turn ends with no more than one unanswered substantive question.

## Core deep-dive sequence for any flagged need

1. What happens?
2. What does another person have to do?
3. How often?
4. How long each time / in total?
5. Why is that help needed?
6. What is likely to happen without it?
7. What is additional compared with a non-disabled child of the same age?
8. Can you give one real representative example?
9. What evidence, if any, supports it?
10. Does it vary, and was it present in the relevant claim/decision period?

Do not ask this as one ten-part question. Select the single most material missing item each turn.

---

# A. Breadth-first screening pass

Use simple neutral questions such as:

### Daytime personal attention
> During an ordinary day, does the child need more hands-on help, prompting, reassurance or one-to-one attention with personal functions than another child the same age?

If no → record no flagged daytime-attention domain and continue.

### Safety supervision
> Does an adult need to monitor or stay ready to intervene because the child would otherwise face a significant safety risk beyond what is usual for their age?

### Treatment/therapy
> Does the child need another person to give, manage, supervise or repeatedly prompt medication, treatment or therapy beyond what is usual for their age?

### Night
> After the household has normally settled for the night, does anyone have to get up, stay awake or repeatedly attend to the child because of their condition or disability?

If no → do not ask a long night battery.

### Physical walking
> Does the child's physical condition substantially limit how far, how fast, for how long or in what manner they can walk outdoors?

### Guidance/supervision outdoors
If child is age-eligible:
> When walking outdoors, does the child need more guidance or supervision from another person than a non-disabled child of the same age?

### School/nursery
> Does the child receive close personal help, one-to-one support, safety supervision or disability-related support at school or nursery that may show additional care or mobility needs?

### Fluctuation
> Are these needs fairly stable, or do they vary substantially across days, weeks, episodes, seasons or treatment cycles?

# B. Daytime functional territories

Only explore where relevant.

## Waking / getting out of bed / starting the day

Possible gaps:
- physical assistance to sit/stand/transfer;
- repeated prompting to wake, rise or initiate self-care;
- disorientation/distress requiring active reassurance;
- seizure/medical monitoring;
- more time or closer assistance than same-age peer.

## Washing, bathing and personal hygiene

Possible gaps:
- hands-on washing/hair/teeth help;
- prompting through every step;
- safety monitoring around water;
- sensory distress requiring active support;
- continence-related cleaning;
- skin/medical care;
- duration and recovery;
- whether ordinary same-age supervision would be materially less.

## Dressing / undressing

Possible gaps:
- physical help with clothing/fastenings/orthoses;
- selection/prompting due to cognitive/sensory issues;
- repeated redressing or regulation support;
- time/episodes;
- same-age difference.

## Toileting / continence

Possible gaps:
- prompting/reminding;
- transfer help;
- wiping/cleaning;
- changing pads/clothes/bedding;
- catheter/stoma/medical procedures;
- accidents and clean-up time;
- daytime versus household-night episodes;
- age comparator especially for younger children.

## Eating / drinking / feeding

Possible gaps:
- physical feeding;
- tube/medical feeding;
- cutting/positioning;
- active prompting/encouragement;
- monitoring choking/aspiration;
- restricted eating/sensory support;
- repeated reassurance to complete intake;
- duration per meal and number of episodes;
- ordinary same-age mealtime support versus additional need.

## Communication / seeing / hearing / understanding

Focus on **personal attention connected to functioning**, not diagnosis alone:
- interpreting communication;
- repeated explanation/prompting;
- sign/communication device support;
- guiding because of vision/hearing difficulty;
- active reassurance/communication needed to complete personal functions;
- frequency and closeness.

## Medication / treatment / therapy

Possible gaps:
- preparing/administering medication;
- inhalers/injections/feeds/oxygen/medical devices;
- monitoring side effects or response;
- seizure rescue medication;
- physiotherapy/OT/SALT exercises requiring another person;
- active prompting versus ordinary age-appropriate reminders;
- frequency/duration/skill;
- school-administered treatment too.

## Pain, fatigue, weakness, dizziness or motor impairment

Ask only about actual care consequences:
- transfers;
- repositioning;
- toileting/bathing/dressing support;
- rest/recovery supervision;
- mobility;
- repeated intervention.

## Executive, cognitive and developmental functioning

Do not equate ADHD/autism/learning disability with entitlement. Ask what another person actually does:
- repeated step-by-step prompting for bodily functions;
- active redirection;
- one-to-one monitoring;
- communication support;
- hazard prevention;
- duration/frequency;
- whether support is substantially beyond same-age range.

## Emotional regulation / distress

Relevant only where it translates into care/supervision needs:
- active reassurance needed to complete bodily functions;
- intervention during dysregulation;
- safety supervision;
- recovery support;
- frequency/duration;
- same-age difference.

Do not turn ordinary emotional support into statutory care without facts.

## Self-injury, aggression, wandering, impulsive danger

Treat neutrally as claim evidence where relevant.

Establish:
- precursor/context;
- exact risk;
- what supervisor does;
- how close they must stay;
- how often intervention is needed;
- actual representative incidents;
- whether supervision is ongoing even when no incident occurs;
- same-age comparison.

Never ask the user to allow an unsafe incident to occur to document it.

## Seizures / blackouts / sudden events

Establish:
- predictability/warning;
- frequency;
- consequences;
- rescue/intervention needed;
- monitoring required between events;
- whether another person must be ready to intervene;
- day/night pattern;
- evidence.

## Equipment / aids / adaptations

For any aid:
- what does it do?
- who sets it up?
- can the child use it alone?
- does an adult still supervise/assist?
- cleaning/maintenance/monitoring burden?
- does it reduce rather than remove need?

# C. Night module

Only run this if night need is flagged.

First establish the household's normal “night” period:

> Roughly when does your household normally settle for the night and when does it normally start the day?

Then build a night ledger. Ask one gap at a time:

- what wakes/alerts the carer or requires them to stay awake?
- what time does it usually happen?
- what does the adult actually do?
- how long does that intervention take?
- how many separate episodes in a night?
- how many nights in a typical week/month or episode cycle?
- must someone remain awake watching even when no active care is being given?
- what danger is being avoided?
- what is ordinary same-age night care versus additional?
- what happens on better nights?
- can a real recent representative night be described?

Do not mechanically apply 20 minutes. Record duration accurately and let the legal analysis consider context.

Commonly forgotten night needs:
- toileting/continence/bed changes;
- repositioning/pain;
- feeds/medication/treatment;
- seizures/respiratory monitoring;
- wandering/unsafe waking;
- prolonged settling **after household night begins**;
- repeated reassurance or active intervention where tied to disability-related need;
- someone awake watching over substantial danger.

# D. Mobility module

## D1. Physical walking / higher-rate territory

Ask about real outdoor walking, not a guessed yardage designed to hit a threshold.

Capture:
- unaided/aided walking;
- distance before severe discomfort or material difficulty;
- speed;
- duration;
- gait/manner;
- stopping/rest;
- falls/instability;
- breathlessness/fatigue/pain/weakness;
- recovery after walking;
- serious deterioration/life-risk concerns;
- wheelchair/buggy use and why;
- what a same-age non-disabled child would ordinarily manage.

Never force the child to walk to failure to measure distance. Use ordinary journeys, clinician/physio measures, school mobility and observed routine.

If mental and physical factors are intertwined, or higher-rate legal route is technical, add adviser flag.

## D2. Guidance/supervision outdoors / lower-rate territory

Capture:
- unfamiliar route behaviour;
- road/traffic awareness;
- orientation/getting lost;
- impulsive running/wandering;
- sensory/communication needs;
- how close the adult stays;
- what prompts/guidance are given;
- interventions to prevent danger;
- frequency across ordinary outdoor walking;
- same-age comparison.

Remember: ability to manage a familiar route does not by itself dispose of the lower-rate statutory test.

---

# E. School / nursery / education

Useful functional questions:

- What adult support is actually provided?
- Is it one-to-one, close/personal, intermittent or general classroom support?
- How often and for how long?
- What bodily function/safety/mobility need is it addressing?
- What would likely happen without it?
- Is support written into an EHCP/IDP/Statement/support plan/risk plan?
- Are incidents logged?
- Does the child appear more independent at school because the environment is structured or support is already built in?
- Does medication timing change presentation?
- Is there post-school dysregulation or recovery that explains home/school difference?

Do not count general teaching or academic help as DLA care without a relevant personal-care/supervision connection.

---

# F. Fluctuation module

Ask for a representative pattern, not only “good” and “bad” labels.

Possible structure:
- stable baseline;
- harder episodes and frequency;
- better periods;
- seasonal/cyclical variation;
- treatment/medication cycles;
- school-day/weekend differences;
- recovery after episodes;
- hospitalisation/acute phases;
- what was true during the relevant decision period.

Do not import PIP's 50%-of-days rule.

---

# G. Same-age comparator prompts

Use only when needed:

> What would another child of the same age normally be expected to do here without this level of adult involvement?

> Is the difference mainly that the child needs help **more often**, for **longer**, more **closely**, with more **skill/vigilance**, or a **kind of help** peers would not normally need?

> If another child this age still needed some adult help, what is different about the amount or nature of help your child needs?

If the user compares with a sibling:

> That comparison is useful as an example, but DLA uses the wider benchmark of a non-disabled child of the same age. What is the extra support your child needs beyond ordinary age-appropriate care?

Never invent “normal milestones” solely to produce a comparator.
