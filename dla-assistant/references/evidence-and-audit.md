# DLA evidence, reconciliation and audit

## 1. Evidence principle

DLA is awarded on **care/mobility needs**, not on the number or prestige of diagnoses.

The parent/carer's detailed factual account is evidence. Professional evidence can corroborate, clarify or independently document need, but the Skill must not imply that a claim is invalid without a new GP letter.

Grade evidence by **what it directly proves**, not by status of the author.

## 2. Evidence directness grades

### Grade A — direct functional evidence

Directly records the relevant care/mobility need, frequency, intervention or risk.

Examples:
- school risk assessment says adult must remain within arm's reach outdoors;
- OT report records hands-on assistance with bathing/transfers;
- care plan specifies overnight repositioning every two hours;
- incident log documents repeated elopement and adult intervention;
- carer diary contemporaneously records repeated night care with times/durations.

### Grade B — contemporaneous functional/contextual evidence

Strongly contextualises the need but may not establish every statutory element.

Examples:
- health journal records a seizure and rescue action;
- school email notes dysregulation and need to leave class with one-to-one adult;
- therapy note records parent reports of feeding support;
- appointment note records recurrent falls.

### Grade C — corroborative impairment evidence

Supports the impairment plausibly causing the claimed need but does not itself prove the help is required.

Examples:
- diagnosis/assessment documenting severe receptive-language difficulty;
- physio report documenting hypotonia or reduced endurance;
- clinician notes documenting epilepsy without describing supervision pattern.

### Grade D — background evidence

Confirms history/condition/treatment but says little about the relevant function.

Examples:
- diagnostic letter with no functional description;
- medication list alone;
- scan result with no care impact.

### Grade E — administrative / low relevance

Examples:
- appointment letters;
- generic condition leaflets;
- duplicated records;
- unrelated investigations.

**Important:** A grade is about directness/relevance, not truthfulness or hierarchy of witnesses. A detailed parent diary can be more probative of night care than a consultant letter that merely names a diagnosis.

## 3. Evidence source labels

Separately label source type:
- parent/carer account;
- child account where appropriate;
- education professional;
- health/social-care professional;
- contemporaneous journal/log;
- third-party witness;
- administrative record.

Also label:
- relevant period: yes/no/uncertain;
- attribution to child: confirmed/uncertain;
- contradiction: none/apparent/material.

## 4. Evidence selection

For submission, prefer a smaller coherent set that establishes the disputed needs.

For each document ask:
1. Which exact need does this support?
2. Is it direct or only contextual?
3. Does it relate to the relevant period?
4. Does it add anything not already established?
5. Does it contain wording likely to look inconsistent and therefore need explanation?
6. Is a short extract/reference enough, or is the whole report required by the process?

Never use “the more pages, the better” as a rule.

## 5. Commonly useful evidence

Depending on jurisdiction/context:
- paediatrician/specialist reports;
- GP/health records where functionally relevant;
- OT/physio/SALT reports;
- nursing/care plans;
- medication/treatment plans;
- school/nursery letters;
- EHCP/IDP/Statement of SEN/current local equivalent;
- one-to-one support plans;
- risk assessments;
- behaviour/incident logs;
- transport/mobility arrangements;
- social-care assessments;
- parent/carer diary;
- relevant emails/messages created in ordinary life;
- evidence of aids/adaptations;
- previous DLA form/award for renewal/challenge context.

## 6. Asking a professional for useful evidence

Do not ask a professional to “say my child qualifies for DLA” or endorse a rate.

Draft a short request asking them to describe what they directly know.

### Template

Subject: Request for factual supporting information about [child]’s day-to-day support needs

Dear [name/team],

I am preparing/challenging a Disability Living Allowance claim for [child]. If you are able to provide a short factual note or copy of an existing relevant record, it would be especially helpful if it could describe the support you have directly observed or arranged, rather than simply restating the diagnosis.

Where relevant, could you include:
- what help/supervision/guidance [child] needs;
- how often and roughly how long it is needed;
- any safety risks and what staff/adults do to manage them;
- personal-care, communication, feeding, toileting, treatment or mobility support;
- any one-to-one or close supervision;
- representative incidents or risk-plan arrangements;
- how this differs from what you would ordinarily expect for children of the same age;
- whether these needs were present around [relevant dates].

Please only include matters you can confirm from your own observations or records. Existing reports/support plans are also useful, so there is no need to write a new letter if the information is already documented.

Many thanks,
[name]

Adapt tone to user. Do not imply the professional is obliged to respond or should copy legal phrases.

## 7. Missing evidence language

Use:

> I couldn't find anything in the records I can access that directly establishes **[specific need/frequency]**. That does not mean the need is absent. It means I should not infer it from a diagnosis or unrelated record. Your own detailed account may still establish it, and we can decide whether another source would add useful corroboration.

Then ask one targeted question.

## 8. Same-age comparison audit

For every major need test:
- what does the child need?
- what would a non-disabled same-age child ordinarily need?
- what is materially additional: frequency, duration, intensity, closeness, skill, vigilance or kind?

Flag statements like:
- “all children need help with that” without analysing degree;
- “my younger child can do it” as sole comparator;
- “they have always been like this” without additional-needs comparison.

## 9. Quantification audit

Build arithmetic where possible:

| Need | Episodes/day or week | Typical duration | Approx total | Source |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---|

Then check:
- do totals conflict across sections?
- does “continual” language fit an account with only isolated moments?
- does “twice a night” match diary/records?
- is an estimated frequency labelled as an estimate?
- are exceptional crisis periods being presented as the ordinary baseline?

Do not reject a need merely because it cannot be precisely timed. Explain uncertainty honestly.

## 10. School-versus-home audit

Potential challenge:
> School describes [child] as independent / settled, while the claim describes extensive support at home.

Do not assume contradiction. Check:
- hidden/built-in school support;
- structured environment;
- reduced demands;
- adult-to-child ratio;
- medication timing;
- masking;
- sensory/environmental differences;
- post-school dysregulation;
- different bodily functions being observed;
- school record language meaning “independent with support”.

Draft the explanation only after user confirms facts.

## 11. Activity/ability consistency audit

Cross-check:
- school attendance;
- clubs/sports;
- travel;
- walking/buggy/wheelchair use;
- sleeping arrangements;
- toileting independence;
- medication self-management;
- communication independence;
- being left alone, if age-appropriate;
- outings and road safety.

Do not treat participation as proof of independence. Ask what support, adaptation, familiarity, recovery or adult input makes it possible.

## 12. MR/appeal reconciliation audit

This is mandatory for challenge work.

Compare:
- original form;
- original evidence;
- decision letter;
- current account;
- MR Notice/department response where available.

For every material difference, label one of:
- clarification of vague original wording;
- omitted detail now recalled;
- normalised need not recognised as “extra” at first;
- form question misunderstood;
- new corroborating evidence for an old need;
- context difference (school/home, medication, environment);
- genuine deterioration/improvement after decision;
- unresolved inconsistency.

Never silently turn an old weak statement into a new absolute statement.

## 13. Devil's Advocate procedure

Ask permission before running full red-team.

For each potential issue present:

### Potential challenge
State the strongest reasonable sceptical reading without hostility.

### Why it matters
Name the legal/factual issue: same-age comparator, frequency, duration, day/night classification, substantial danger, mobility route, relevant period, etc.

### Evidence currently available
List only what the record actually supports.

### Plausible non-contradictory explanations
List only possibilities to explore, not facts.

### One resolving question
Ask the single most useful question.

### Resolution
After user answers, either:
- amend the draft;
- add an explanation;
- downgrade confidence;
- leave as unresolved vulnerability.

## 14. Pre-submission challenge list

Test:
- vague “lots/often/sometimes” terms;
- absent duration;
- absent same-age comparison;
- diagnosis-only reasoning;
- generic parenting tasks with no additional element;
- missing night module despite sleep/night evidence;
- night care incorrectly timed before household night;
- supervision claim with no identified danger/intervention;
- danger claim that is remote/trivial/unexplained;
- lower mobility claim based only on familiar route difficulty or general reassurance;
- higher mobility claim with no physical/legal route analysis;
- school evidence apparently contradicting home;
- too much irrelevant evidence;
- records that refer to another family member;
- evidence from wrong period;
- MR/appeal retrofit.

## 15. Escalate flags

Recommend a welfare-rights/legal adviser check without abandoning factual preparation where there is:
- severe mental impairment/severe behavioural problems higher-mobility route;
- capacity/appointeeship dispute;
- looked-after/local-authority care payment issue;
- complex residence/export/public-funds issue;
- hospital/residential-accommodation payment rules;
- disputed legal interpretation/case law;
- risk that challenging an existing award could materially reduce entitlement;
- Upper Tribunal/point-of-law issue.
