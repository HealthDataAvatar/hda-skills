# Child DLA rules and tests

Last verified: **30 August 2026**. Current authoritative law/guidance overrides this file.

## 1. Basic architecture

For a new child DLA claim, the child is under 16 and has qualifying additional **care** and/or **mobility** needs. A formal diagnosis is not itself the test; DWP's DMG expressly recognises that entitlement can arise from functional needs even without a settled diagnostic label.

The ordinary disability-duration rule is that the relevant difficulties have existed for at least **3 months** and are expected to continue for at least **6 months**. Special Rules for End of Life are an important exception and must be screened early.

Residence/presence/public-funds rules can be complex. If there has been a recent move, international residence, immigration restriction, export situation or cross-border move, verify current official rules rather than relying on a summary.

## 2. The child comparator: the central DLA test

For a child under 16, it is not enough to show that the child needs care. Non-disabled children also need care.

The relevant comparison is with **a child of the same age in normal physical and mental health / a non-disabled child of the same age**.

The child can satisfy the additional-needs test where they require:

- substantially more care/guidance/supervision than children of the same age would normally require; or
- care/guidance/supervision of a kind that a younger child might ordinarily need but a non-disabled child of this child's age would not.

Important:
- do not compare only with a sibling;
- do not compare only with the child's own younger self;
- do not ask “what would this particular child need if they did not have the condition?” as the legal comparator;
- account for the wide normal range at a given age;
- compare **quantity, duration, intensity, closeness, skill and type of help**, not only whether some help exists.

Decision-maker guidance also emphasises that what matters is what help is **required**, not simply what is actually provided.

## 3. Care component

The care component has three rates, but before a decision the Skill should explain the architecture rather than predict a rate.

### Day condition

Broadly, the day condition concerns a need for either:

- **frequent attention throughout the day in connection with bodily functions**, or
- **continual supervision throughout the day to avoid substantial danger** to the child or others.

### Night condition

Broadly, the night condition concerns a need at night for either:

- **prolonged or repeated attention in connection with bodily functions**, or
- another person to be **awake for a prolonged period or at frequent intervals** for the purpose of watching over the child to avoid substantial danger.

### Lowest-rate care territory

For an under-16 child, the relevant route is a need for **attention in connection with bodily functions for a significant portion of the day**, subject to the child comparator.

### Rate architecture

- middle-rate care broadly corresponds to satisfying the day **or** night condition;
- highest-rate care broadly corresponds to satisfying both day **and** night conditions, with separate Special Rules provisions;
- lowest-rate care has the significant-portion daytime attention route for children.

Do not quote current money rates from this file; verify live if the user asks.

## 4. Attention in connection with bodily functions

“Attention” is not synonymous with hands-on physical care. Decision-maker guidance treats it as a personal service of an active nature connected with bodily functions.

Bodily functions are interpreted broadly and can include functions such as:
- eating/drinking;
- washing/bathing;
- dressing/undressing;
- toileting/elimination;
- getting into/out of bed, sitting, walking;
- seeing/hearing/communication connected with functioning;
- sleeping;
- brain/mental functions where active personal intervention is required.

Prompting, encouragement, communication assistance or reassurance **can** amount to relevant attention when it is an active personal service connected to the bodily function. Do not mechanically count every verbal reminder as qualifying attention; establish what the person actually does, why it is needed, how close/personal it is, and its frequency/duration.

Purely domestic tasks are not automatically “attention”. A domestic action can matter where it is closely associated with a bodily function in a continuous episode of personal care. Explain this distinction in “What is this question really asking?” mode.

## 5. Frequent / throughout the day

Do not convert these terms into a rigid arithmetic rule. Decision-maker guidance treats “frequent” contextually; several separate episodes may be relevant, and the whole-day pattern matters.

Interview for:
- number of episodes;
- spread across the day;
- duration;
- intensity;
- whether the intervention is actually required;
- how this compares with same-age ordinary care.

## 6. Continual supervision / substantial danger

“Continual” is not necessarily literally uninterrupted visual observation. The key issue is an ongoing need for another person to be in a position to intervene, allowing for ordinary brief interruptions.

Establish:
- the danger/risk;
- likelihood and seriousness;
- why the child cannot reliably avoid it without supervision;
- how close/active the supervisor must be;
- how much of the day the need exists;
- actual representative incidents, without requiring unsafe testing;
- same-age comparison.

Examples can include wandering, traffic danger, serious falls, seizures/blackouts, unsafe impulsive acts, self-injury or danger to others, but never infer entitlement from a condition/category alone.

A remote or trivial possibility is not enough; the risk must be materially relevant under the statutory test.

## 7. Night — technical definition

Do not equate “night” with “after the child goes to bed”.

DWP decision-maker guidance describes night as the household's period of inactivity in the dark hours, with an objective element. Care provided after the child goes to bed but while the household is still normally up may count as **day**, not night.

Before classifying night care, ask:
- when the household normally settles for the night;
- when it normally gets up;
- when each intervention occurs;
- whether the carer has to be awake;
- how long each episode lasts;
- how often it occurs.

## 8. Prolonged / repeated / frequent intervals at night

Decision-maker guidance says:
- “repeated” means more than once and not merely a one-off/occasional requirement;
- for “prolonged”, around 20 minutes is a decision-maker starting point, **not an absolute legal threshold**; shorter attention can still be prolonged on the facts;
- “frequent intervals” need not be evenly spread across the whole night.

Never tell a user that a 19-minute episode “doesn't count” or that 20 minutes guarantees entitlement.

## 9. Significant portion of the day

This is fact-sensitive, not a hard timer. Decision-maker guidance says “significant” means more than negligible or trivial and notes that around an hour can be reasonable in many cases, while less may suffice where there are many small disruptive episodes or the attention is particularly intense.

Use duration as evidence, not as a mechanical pass/fail threshold.

## 10. Period throughout which / fluctuation

Decision-maker guidance directs a broad view across the relevant period rather than requiring a particular number of days/nights per week in every case.

For fluctuating conditions:
- capture ordinary distribution across the period;
- quantify frequency where possible;
- distinguish isolated exceptional events from recurring need;
- explain cyclical/episodic patterns;
- do not draft only the child's worst day.

## 11. School/nursery evidence

Support at school can be highly relevant. Close/personal attention in the classroom may count where it is connected with bodily functions, and guidance/supervision evidence can illuminate the child's needs.

School and home may legitimately differ because of:
- structure;
- one-to-one support;
- environmental demands;
- masking/holding together then dysregulating later;
- medication timing;
- sensory load;
- familiarity;
- reduced expectations or adaptations.

Never treat “school says fine” or “home says difficult” as dispositive. Investigate the context.

## 12. Mobility component

There are two legally different routes. Keep them separate.

### Lower-rate mobility territory

Age threshold: normally **5 or over**.

This is for a child who can walk but, because of physical or mental disability, needs **guidance or supervision from another person most of the time when walking outdoors**, subject to the same-age additional-needs test.

Decision-maker guidance says ability to use **familiar routes is disregarded** for this test.

Interview for:
- guidance versus supervision;
- unfamiliar/outdoor routes;
- danger awareness;
- road safety;
- orientation/getting lost;
- communication/sensory issues affecting safe outdoor walking;
- what the accompanying adult actually does;
- how this exceeds same-age ordinary supervision.

Do not equate general anxiety/reassurance automatically with statutory guidance/supervision; clarify the functional need.

### Higher-rate mobility territory

Age threshold: normally **3 or over**.

Current law contains several routes, including:
- unable or virtually unable to walk because of physical disability;
- severe visual impairment criteria;
- deaf-and-blind criteria;
- specified severe mental impairment + severe behavioural problem + highest-care conditions;
- specified bilateral lower-limb-loss/equivalent routes.

For the physical “virtually unable to walk” route, decision-maker guidance considers distance, speed, time and manner of walking without severe discomfort, and whether the effort of walking would endanger life or cause serious deterioration in health.

These routes are legally technical. Never collapse them into “struggles walking”.

Add **ESCALATE: welfare-rights/legal adviser check** for:
- severe mental impairment/severe behavioural problems route;
- complex physical/mental causation questions;
- severe visual/hearing criteria;
- serious deterioration/life-risk walking route where legal interpretation is contested.

## 13. Aids, adaptations and strategies

Record aids/adaptations/strategies actually used and what they achieve.

Ask:
- can the child use the aid independently?
- does it remove the need for personal attention/supervision, or merely reduce it?
- is an adult still needed to prompt, fit, clean, monitor or manage it?
- what happens when the strategy is unavailable?

Do not invent a theoretical aid and then assume it removes entitlement.

## 14. Special Rules for End of Life

If a medical professional has said the child might have **12 months or less to live**, check the current Special Rules route immediately.

England/Wales current official route: DLA child claim + SR1 process; highest care component is available under the special rules, with mobility considered separately.

Northern Ireland current official route: DLA1 + SR1, with faster handling under special rules.

Do not require the ordinary 3-month past-needs period before routing Special Rules.

Do not quote monetary amounts from this file because rates change and official pages can lag during uprating transitions.

## 15. Before-decision rate discipline

Explain legal architecture, but do not say:
- “this should get middle rate”;
- “you qualify for highest rate”;
- “this is worth X”.

Instead say:
- “your evidence currently addresses daytime frequent attention”;
- “night needs have not yet been established”;
- “this appears relevant to lower-rate mobility guidance/supervision territory”; or
- “the higher-rate route is legally technical and needs specialist checking”.

After a real decision, it is appropriate to compare the decision with the legal tests to identify grounds for challenge.
