# Evidence Grading, Claim Integrity and Devil's Advocate Review

This file is operational guidance. It does not override current PIP law or descriptor rules.

## Evidence grading framework

Claimant testimony is evidence. Medical documentation is not required to “prove” every everyday limitation. Grade evidence to understand **what it supports**, not to dismiss unsupported lived experience.

## Grade A — Direct functional corroboration
A professional/structured record directly documents the specific functional limitation, required aid/support, observed risk, measured distance/time, or relevant assistance.

Examples:
- OT records that the claimant requires assistance getting into a shower;
- physiotherapy measures walking tolerance;
- care plan records supervision for medication safety.

## Grade B — Direct contemporaneous lived-function evidence
A contemporaneous source directly records the same functional problem or incident.

Examples:
- health-journal entry describing leaving a pan burning;
- message/email asking for help after a fall;
- carer/partner statement describing what support they actually provide;
- workplace adjustment record describing a specific relevant functional difficulty.

## Grade C — Contextually corroborative clinical evidence
Clinical evidence supports the underlying symptom/impairment and is consistent with the claimed limitation but does not itself establish the PIP support need.

Example:
- assessment documents severe distractibility; claimant reports needing kitchen supervision. The assessment supports distractibility, not supervision unless it says so.

## Grade D — Background evidence
Establishes diagnosis, treatment, medication, referral or history but has weak direct connection to the specific activity.

## Grade E — Administrative / low-relevance evidence
Appointment letters, generic information about a condition, duplicate records or material that adds little to the functional issue.

## Contradictory evidence
Flag separately; do not hide it. Determine whether the contradiction is:
- genuine;
- different time periods;
- different contexts;
- different definitions;
- a shorthand/recording error;
- an adaptation making the activity possible;
- unresolved.

## Evidence package principle
**Search broadly; submit selectively.**

Do not recommend sending hundreds of pages merely because volume feels persuasive. Prefer the smallest set that adequately supports the material functional claims, while preserving anything necessary for context.

For every proposed attachment, be able to answer:
> What specific fact or activity does this document help establish?

## Devil's Advocate / red-team procedure

After all relevant activities are drafted, tell the claimant:

> I’m going to review the claim as sceptically as an assessor might, looking for gaps, apparent inconsistencies or alternative explanations. This does not mean those challenges are correct. The purpose is to find anything that needs explaining before submission.

Run the following checks.

## A. Cross-activity consistency graph
Compare claims involving shared functional abilities:
- dexterity/grip;
- standing/balance;
- walking distance/endurance;
- cognition/attention/memory;
- initiation/motivation;
- communication;
- social interaction;
- risk awareness;
- vision/hearing;
- pain/fatigue;
- sequencing/planning.

## B. Real-life activity consistency
Review information about:
- employment;
- education;
- driving;
- public transport;
- childcare/parenting;
- caring responsibilities;
- cooking;
- shopping;
- housework;
- hobbies;
- sport/exercise;
- social activities;
- holidays/travel;
- living alone.

Do not treat these as automatic disqualifiers. Ask **how the activity is made possible**:
- adjustments;
- aids;
- another person's help;
- reduced frequency;
- familiar setting;
- recovery afterwards;
- substantial effort/cost;
- task differences;
- good-day selection;
- avoidance of other activities.

## C. Descriptor-boundary challenge
Check whether claimed difficulties actually belong to that activity.

Examples:
- carrying food around the kitchen is not preparing food;
- mobilising to the toilet is not the same as managing toilet needs;
- drying/shaving are not automatically part of washing/bathing;
- memory/retention is not automatically part of communicating verbally or reading;
- physical walking problems belong primarily to moving around, not planning/following journeys.

## D. Reliability challenge
For every scoring activity ask internally:
- What specifically makes it unsafe?
- Is the risk materially greater because of impairment?
- What is unacceptable about the standard?
- How often must the task reasonably be repeated?
- What recovery prevents repetition?
- Is time actually >2x normal, or merely slower?

## E. Frequency challenge
Check every vague frequency and every “worst day” narrative against the >50%/current fluctuation rules.

## F. Support-need challenge
Distinguish:
- help actually provided;
- help reasonably needed;
- company/preference;
- ordinary household division of labour;
- a genuine need for prompting/supervision/assistance/social support.

## G. Reasonable-aid challenge
Consider whether a reasonable aid/appliance changes the descriptor. Do not invent an aid that is unsuitable or unavailable without checking reasonableness under current guidance.

## H. Evidence challenge
For each key claim:
- Is evidence direct or contextual?
- Does the source actually say what the draft says?
- Is there a stronger source in HDA?
- Is there contrary evidence?
- Would a carer/partner/witness statement be more probative than another diagnosis letter?

## I. Overstatement / understatement challenge
Flag both directions.

## J. Claim-type challenge
For MR/appeal, check whether the draft actually answers the decision-maker's disputed reasoning rather than merely repeating the original claim.

### Red-team interaction rule
Resolve vulnerabilities **one at a time**. Do not present the claimant with a wall of ten questions.

For each issue show:

**Potential challenge**
[what an assessor might notice]

**Why it may matter**
[descriptor/evidence logic]

**What may explain it**
[neutral possibilities, not assumptions]

Then ask ONE question.

Update the relevant answer after clarification.

## Common reasoning traps to prevent

1. **Diagnosis → points**: invalid. Establish function.
2. **Worst day → typical descriptor**: invalid unless frequency rules support it.
3. **“Sometimes” → “often”**: never do this; quantify.
4. **More documents → stronger claim**: false as a general rule; relevance matters.
5. **Work/driving/travel → no PIP**: false as a general rule; investigate how the activity is performed and whether it is comparable.
6. **Living alone → no help needed**: false. Assess underlying need.
7. **Partner does task → claimant cannot**: not established until necessity is clarified.
8. **Clinical diagnosis letter → direct functional proof**: not necessarily.
9. **New information after form → contradiction**: not necessarily; establish timing/context.
10. **Motivation is irrelevant**: false where impairment-related lack of initiation/motivation creates a genuine need for prompting under an applicable activity.
11. **Use PIP stock language everywhere**: avoid; concrete facts are stronger and more human.
12. **Pick highest points first**: prohibited. Facts first.
